Access Control
Access Control Board: Installation and Configuration
By Shammi Alam Shaikh · 11 Nov 2026 · 9 min read
An access control system (ACS) decides who can open which door and when. The brain of each door, or group of doors, is the controller board (also called a panel). This article explains how the parts work together and the usual steps to install and configure them.



Safety first: Wiring is different for each brand and model. Always follow the manufacturer's installation manual and wiring diagram. Work on mains power only if you are qualified, and follow local fire and building rules for doors on escape routes.



Parts of the system


    
  
  • Controller board: holds the rules, makes the decision, and has inputs and relay outputs.
    • 
  
  • Reader: reads the credential: card, key fob, PIN, fingerprint, face or phone.
    • 
  
  • Lock: an electric strike or a magnetic lock (mag lock).
    • 
  
  • Door contact: a magnetic sensor that tells if the door is open or closed.
    • 
  
  • REX (request to exit): a button or motion sensor inside that lets people leave without a card.
    • 
  
  • Power supply with battery backup: gives the right voltage, normally 12 V or 24 V DC, and keeps doors working during a power cut.
    • 
  
  • Software or server: where you add doors, cardholders and rules, and where events are stored.
    • 




How the decision is made



  Card→Reader→Controller→Relay→Lock opens



    
  
  1. The reader sends the card number to the controller.
    2. 
  
  2. The controller checks its stored list: is the card valid, is it allowed at this door, and is the time within the schedule?
    3. 
  
  3. If yes, it energizes the relay to release the lock for a set time, for example 5 seconds, and records the event.
    4. 
  
  4. If no, the door stays locked and the denied attempt is recorded.
    5. 




Fail-safe and fail-secure locks


    
  
  • Fail-safe (for example a mag lock): the door unlocks when power is lost. It is used on escape routes.
    • 
  
  • Fail-secure (for example many electric strikes): the door stays locked when power is lost, but can still be opened from the inside by the handle.
    • 



Doors on escape routes must also release when the fire alarm triggers. This is usually done by connecting a relay from the fire alarm system into the lock's power line. This is a life-safety requirement, so it must follow local rules.



Installation: how things are connected


The exact terminal names vary, so use these as a general idea only.



  
PartConnects toNotes

  
ReaderController reader portPower (+12 V and ground) plus data. Data is Wiegand (D0 and D1 wires) or OSDP (RS-485, two wires A and B), plus LED and buzzer wires on some models.

  
LockController relay output (COM, NO, NC)Use a separate power supply for the lock if the manual says so. Add a diode or suppression across the lock to protect the relay.

  
Door contactController inputTells the system if the door is open, closed, held open or forced.

  
REX buttonController inputLets people exit and tells the system it was a valid exit.

  
ControllerNetwork (Ethernet) or RS-485 busGives it an IP address or bus address so the software can find it.

  
Tamper switchController inputAlarms if the enclosure or reader is opened.




    
  
  • Use shielded multi-core cable. Run it away from mains cables to avoid noise.
    • 
  
  • Wiegand cable is commonly limited to about 150 m. OSDP works over much longer distance and supports encryption, so it is the better choice for new installations.
    • 
  
  • Mount the controller and power supply in a locked, secure enclosure on the secure side of the door, never next to the reader outside.
    • 
  
  • Label every cable at both ends.
    • 




Configuration steps


    
  
  1. Power on the controller and check the status lights.
    2. 
  
  2. Set the controller's network or bus address. Give it a static IP address if it uses Ethernet.
    3. 
  
  3. Add the controller to the access control software using its address, and check it shows as online.
    4. 
  
  4. Define the door: name it, and select the reader, the lock relay, the door contact input and the REX input.
    5. 
  
  5. Set door timing: unlock time (for example 5 s), and held-open time (how long the door may stay open before an alarm).
    6. 
  
  6. Create schedules: for example office hours, weekdays only.
    7. 
  
  7. Create access levels (groups): which doors a group can open and at what times, such as Staff, Contractors, Server room.
    8. 
  
  8. Add cardholders and credentials: enter names, enroll or type the card numbers and assign access levels.
    9. 
  
  9. Set alarms: door forced open, door held open, tamper, and repeated denied attempts.
    10. 
  
  10. Test everything: a valid card, an invalid card, a valid card outside the schedule, the exit button, door held open, forced door, power cut with battery, and fire alarm release.
    11. 
  
  11. Save a backup of the configuration and keep a record of the wiring.
    12. 




Common problems


    
  
  • Reader shows no light: check the power wires and the voltage at the reader.
    • 
  
  • Card is read but door does not unlock: check the relay wiring (COM and NO or NC), the lock's power supply, and that the card has access at that time.
    • 
  
  • Wrong or random card numbers: D0 and D1 may be swapped, or the card format in the software does not match the card.
    • 
  
  • Controller offline: check the IP address, cable and switch port.
    • 
  
  • Door alarm always on: the door contact is badly aligned, or the input is set as normally open instead of normally closed.
    •

Data Center
How a Data Center Works
By Shammi Alam Shaikh · 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read
A data center is a building, or a part of a building, that holds the servers, storage and network equipment needed to run applications and store data. Banks, hospitals, telecom companies, cloud providers and large companies all depend on them. The goal is simple: keep the equipment running all the time, safely and cooled.



The five systems inside a data center


    
  
  1. Compute and storage: servers and storage arrays mounted in racks.
    2. 
  
  2. Network: switches, routers, firewalls and cabling that connect everything together and to the outside world.
    3. 
  
  3. Power: supplies electricity without interruption.
    4. 
  
  4. Cooling: removes the heat that equipment produces.
    5. 
  
  5. Physical security and monitoring: access control, cameras, fire protection and sensors.
    6. 




Racks and rack units


Equipment is mounted in standard 19-inch racks. Height is measured in rack units (U). One U is 1.75 inches (44.45 mm). A common rack is 42U tall, so it can hold, for example, 42 servers that are 1U each. Rows of racks are arranged with the front of the equipment facing a cold aisle and the back facing a hot aisle.



Power



  Utility power→Transfer switch→UPS→PDU→Rack equipment



    
  
  • UPS (uninterruptible power supply): batteries that carry the load instantly when utility power fails, and clean the power.
    • 
  
  • Generator: starts within seconds and supplies power for hours or days, while the UPS bridges the short gap.
    • 
  
  • PDU (power distribution unit): distributes power to the equipment inside racks. Servers usually have two power supplies connected to two separate power paths (A and B).
    • 




Cooling


Servers turn almost all the electricity they use into heat. Cooling units (CRAC or CRAH) send cold air into the cold aisle. Servers pull it in from the front and push hot air out of the back. Hot aisle or cold aisle containment uses walls or curtains so hot and cold air do not mix, which saves energy. Large sites also use chilled water and, in some cases, liquid cooling for very dense equipment.


Efficiency is measured by PUE (power usage effectiveness): total facility power divided by the power used by IT equipment. A PUE of 1.0 would mean no energy is wasted on cooling or losses. Lower is better.



Network design: spine and leaf


Older data centers used three layers: access, distribution and core. Most modern data centers use spine-leaf.


    
  
  • Leaf switches are usually at the top of each rack and connect to the servers.
    • 
  
  • Spine switches connect to every leaf switch.
    • 
  
  • Any server can reach any other server in the same number of hops (leaf, spine, leaf), so speed is predictable and it is easy to add capacity by adding more leaf or spine switches.
    • 




Redundancy: N, N+1 and 2N


    
  
  • N: only what is needed. If one part fails, there is an outage.
    • 
  
  • N+1: one extra unit. One failure is covered.
    • 
  
  • 2N: a complete second copy of the whole system.
    • 




Tier levels


The Uptime Institute describes data center reliability in four tiers. The availability figures are the commonly quoted targets.



  
TierDescriptionAvailability

  
IBasic, single path, no redundancy99.671%

  
IISome redundant components99.741%

  
IIIConcurrently maintainable, can be serviced without shutdown99.982%

  
IVFault tolerant, survives a failure of any single system99.995%





Virtualization and cloud


Instead of one application per physical server, virtualization runs many virtual machines on one server, so hardware is used better. Cloud providers build huge data centers and rent this capacity by the hour. Many companies keep some systems in their own data center and some in the cloud, which is called a hybrid setup.



Physical security


Data centers use layers of protection: fences, guards, access control at every door, mantraps, CCTV cameras, and rack-level locks. Fire protection uses early smoke detection and gas-based or water mist suppression, so equipment is not damaged by water. Environmental sensors watch temperature, humidity and water leaks.

Security software
How Genetec Works
By Shammi Alam Shaikh · 11 Nov 2026 · 7 min read
Genetec is a company that makes security software. Its main product, Security Center, is a unified platform. This means video surveillance, access control and other security systems are managed from one place, with one database and one operator screen, instead of separate programs.



Note: Genetec renames modules and releases new versions often. Use this article to understand the idea, and check the official Genetec documentation for the exact version you install.



The main modules


    
  
  • Video surveillance (Omnicast): connects to IP cameras and encoders, shows live video and records it.
    • 
  
  • Access control (Synergis): manages doors, readers, cardholders and access rights through door controllers.
    • 
  
  • License plate recognition (AutoVu): reads vehicle number plates from special cameras.
    • 



Because they share one platform, an event in one module can trigger an action in another. For example, a forced door can automatically show the nearest camera to the operator.



Roles: the building blocks of the system


Security Center is made of roles. Each role does one job and can run on its own server or together with other roles on one server.



  
RoleWhat it does

  
DirectoryThe main server. Keeps the configuration database, users and licences, and tells every other part where to connect. Can have a failover server.

  
ArchiverConnects to cameras, records video to storage and serves it back for playback.

  
Access ManagerTalks to access control units. Keeps cardholders and access rules in sync with the controllers.

  
Media RouterFinds the best network path to send video from a camera or Archiver to an operator.

  
FederationJoins several separate Security Center systems, such as different sites, so one operator can see them all.





The two main applications


    
  
  • Config Tool: used by the administrator to add cameras, doors, users, schedules and rules.
    • 
  
  • Security Desk: used by the security operator to watch live video, play back recordings, handle alarms and search events.
    • 




How video flows



  IP camera→Archiver→Storage→Security Desk



The camera sends its video stream to the Archiver. The Archiver saves it according to the recording schedule (continuous, on motion or on event). When an operator opens a camera in Security Desk, the Directory and Media Router decide the best route and the video is streamed to the operator's screen.



How an access control event flows


    
  
  1. A person presents a card at the reader.
    2. 
  
  2. The door controller checks the card against its local copy of the access rules and unlocks the door if allowed. It does not need the server for this, so doors keep working if the network is down.
    3. 
  
  3. The controller sends the event (access granted or denied) to the Access Manager.
    4. 
  
  4. The Access Manager stores it through the Directory, and Security Desk shows it live to the operator.
    5. 




Important concepts


    
  
  • Entities: everything in the system (a camera, a door, a user, an area) is an entity with its own settings and permissions.
    • 
  
  • Events and actions: when something happens (an event), the system can run an automatic action, such as send an email, start recording or show a camera.
    • 
  
  • Alarms: events that need an operator to acknowledge and respond.
    • 
  
  • Schedules: time rules used for recording, access and alarms.
    • 
  
  • Privileges: each user can only see and control what the administrator allows.
    • 




What the network must provide


    
  
  • Stable IP addresses for servers, cameras and controllers.
    • 
  
  • Enough bandwidth for all video streams. This is the biggest load on the network.
    • 
  
  • Correct time on all devices using NTP, so recordings and events match.
    • 
  
  • Regular backups of the Directory database and enough storage for recordings.
    •

Networking
How a Network Works
By Shammi Alam Shaikh · 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read
A network is a group of devices connected together so they can share data. The devices can be computers, phones, printers, IP cameras or servers. The connection can be a cable or Wi-Fi. Everything on the internet, from a WhatsApp message to a video call, travels as small pieces of data called packets.



The main parts of a network


    
  
  • End devices: the devices that send and receive data, such as laptops, phones, servers and cameras.
    • 
  
  • Switch: connects devices inside one local network (LAN). It learns which device is on which port and sends data only to the right port.
    • 
  
  • Router: connects different networks together, for example your office LAN to the internet. It chooses the best path for each packet.
    • 
  
  • Firewall: allows or blocks traffic using rules. It protects the network from unwanted access.
    • 
  
  • Access point: gives Wi-Fi access to devices without cables.
    • 
  
  • Cables: copper (Cat5e or Cat6, up to 100 m) and fiber (for long distance and high speed).
    • 





  Laptop→Switch→Router→Internet→Server




Addresses: how devices find each other


Every device has more than one kind of address, and each one has its own job.


    
  
  • MAC address: a hardware address of the network card. It is used inside the local network (Layer 2).
    • 
  
  • IP address: a logical address such as 192.168.10.25. It is used to reach devices across networks (Layer 3). IP addresses can be given by hand (static) or automatically by a DHCP server.
    • 
  
  • Subnet mask: tells a device which part of the IP address is the network and which part is the host. For 192.168.10.25/24, the network is 192.168.10.0 and hosts go from .1 to .254.
    • 
  
  • Default gateway: the router address that a device uses when the destination is outside its own network.
    • 
  
  • Port number: identifies the application. Web traffic uses port 80 (HTTP) or 443 (HTTPS).
    • 




What happens when you open a website


    
  
  1. You type a name like example.com. Your device asks a DNS server to convert the name into an IP address.
    2. 
  
  2. Your device checks if that IP is in its own subnet. It is not, so it sends the packet to the default gateway.
    3. 
  
  3. To reach the gateway inside the LAN, it uses ARP to find the gateway's MAC address.
    4. 
  
  4. The switch forwards the frame to the router using the MAC address table.
    5. 
  
  5. The router replaces your private IP with its public IP using NAT and forwards the packet to the internet.
    6. 
  
  6. Routers on the way look at the destination IP and pass the packet along until it reaches the web server.
    7. 
  
  7. The server replies, and the same path is followed back to you. Your browser shows the page.
    8. 




The OSI model


The OSI model splits networking into seven layers. It helps to understand and troubleshoot problems step by step.



  
LayerNameWhat it doesExample

  
7ApplicationServices the user usesHTTP, DNS, SMTP

  
6PresentationFormat, encryption, compressionTLS, JPEG

  
5SessionStarts and ends conversationsLogin sessions

  
4TransportEnd-to-end delivery, portsTCP, UDP

  
3NetworkIP addressing and routingIP, router

  
2Data linkDelivery inside a LAN, MAC addressEthernet, switch

  
1PhysicalCables, signals, bitsCat6, fiber





TCP and UDP


TCP checks that every packet arrives, in order, and resends lost ones. It is used for web pages, email and file transfer. UDP is faster because it does not check, so it is used for live video, voice calls and games, where a small loss is acceptable.



VLANs


A VLAN splits one physical switch into several separate logical networks. For example, office computers can be in VLAN 10, cameras in VLAN 20 and servers in VLAN 30. This improves security and keeps traffic organized. Traffic between VLANs must pass through a router or a Layer 3 switch.



Troubleshooting tip: Start from the bottom. Check the cable and link light (Layer 1), then the IP address and gateway (Layer 3), then DNS and the application. Most problems are found in the first two steps.

CCTV
How to Configure an IP Camera
By Shammi Alam Shaikh · 11 Nov 2026 · 8 min read
An IP camera sends video over the network instead of through a separate video cable. It has its own IP address and a small web page for settings. A proper configuration makes the camera reliable, gives good video quality and keeps it secure.



What you need


    
  
  • The IP camera and a PoE switch (power over Ethernet) or a PoE injector. A single Cat5e/Cat6 cable gives both power and data, up to 100 m.
    • 
  
  • A laptop connected to the same network.
    • 
  
  • An NVR (network video recorder) or video management software such as Genetec.
    • 
  
  • The camera's manual and its default login details.
    • 
  
  • A plan for IP addresses: which range is for cameras, the subnet mask and the gateway.
    • 




Step-by-step configuration


    
  
  1. Connect and power the camera. Plug it into a PoE port. Wait a minute for it to start.
    2. 
  
  2. Find the camera's IP address. Use the manufacturer's discovery tool, or check the DHCP list on the router. Some cameras ship with a fixed default address, so set your laptop to the same subnet if the manual says so.
    3. 
  
  3. Log in through a web browser and change the default password immediately. Use a strong password and keep it in a safe place.
    4. 
  
  4. Set a static IP address. Enter the IP address, subnet mask, default gateway and DNS from your plan. Cameras should keep the same address so the recorder can always find them. Write each camera's address in a list.
    5. 
  
  5. Set the date and time. Point the camera to an NTP time server and set the correct time zone. Wrong time makes recordings useless as evidence.
    6. 
  
  6. Set the video stream. See the section below.
    7. 
  
  7. Set the image. Adjust focus and zoom, brightness, contrast, wide dynamic range (WDR) for bright backlight, and night mode (IR). Add a camera name or a time stamp overlay if needed.
    8. 
  
  8. Set motion detection or analytics if you want recording or alerts only when something moves. Draw the detection area and adjust sensitivity.
    9. 
  
  9. Add the camera to the NVR or software. Enter the IP address, port, username and password, and choose the protocol: the manufacturer's own driver, ONVIF (a common standard that lets different brands work together) or an RTSP stream address.
    10. 
  
  10. Test. Check live view, start a recording, play it back, and check the time stamp is correct.
    11. 




Video stream settings


    
  
  • Codec: H.264 is widely supported. H.265 gives similar quality at a lower bitrate, so it needs less storage, but the recorder must support it.
    • 
  
  • Resolution: for example 1080p (2 MP) or 4 MP. Higher resolution shows more detail but uses more bandwidth and storage.
    • 
  
  • Frame rate: 12–15 fps is usually enough for normal surveillance. Use more only where fast movement matters.
    • 
  
  • Bitrate: the amount of data per second. Constant bitrate (CBR) is predictable. Variable bitrate (VBR) saves storage when the scene is quiet.
    • 
  
  • Main stream and sub stream: the main stream is high quality for recording. The sub stream is lower quality for viewing many cameras at once on one screen.
    • 




Planning storage


A simple estimate for one camera for one day:


Storage per day (GB) = bitrate (Mbps) × 3600 × 24 ÷ 8 ÷ 1000
Example: a camera at 4 Mbps uses 4 × 86,400 ÷ 8 = 43,200 MB, about 43 GB per day. For 16 cameras kept 30 days: 43 × 16 × 30 ≈ 20.6 TB, so plan more than that for safety.


Bandwidth also matters: 16 cameras at 4 Mbps send 64 Mbps to the recorder, so the switch ports and uplinks must handle it.



PoE basics


    
  
  • 802.3af gives up to 15.4 W per port, 802.3at (PoE+) up to 30 W, and 802.3bt up to 60 W or more.
    • 
  
  • Cameras with heaters, motors (PTZ) or strong IR need more power. Check the camera's watt rating and the switch's total PoE budget.
    • 




Security of the camera network


    
  
  • Put cameras in their own VLAN, separate from office computers.
    • 
  
  • Do not open camera ports directly to the internet. Use a VPN for remote viewing.
    • 
  
  • Turn off services you do not use, update the firmware, and use a unique strong password for each system.
    • 




Common problems: no picture (wrong IP, subnet or password), picture freezes (not enough bandwidth or a bad cable), wrong time (no NTP), recorder cannot add the camera (ONVIF turned off or wrong port).
